Scene One · The Mist

Unseen through a hostile city

While Nausicaä drives her wagon into the city and reaches her father’s palace, Odysseus waits behind in the grove, exactly as she told him. When he judges enough time has passed, he rises and walks toward the walls of the Phaeacian city, praying under his breath that the welcome he receives will be kind.

Athena does not leave him to walk in alone. She wraps him in a thick mist, so that not one Phaeacian in the crowded streets will see him, challenge him, or ask his business before he reaches the palace. She knows her people well: the Phaeacians are wary of strangers, and a foreign face draws suspicion long before it draws hospitality.

She takes the shape of a young girl carrying a bronze water pitcher and meets him at the edge of the city. When Odysseus asks the way to the house of Alcinous, she agrees to guide him — but not before giving him one hard instruction.

“Walk quickly, stranger, and follow close behind me. Look at no one, and ask no one any questions. These people have little patience for outsiders, and they do not welcome a foreign face kindly.”

They pass through streets thick with Phaeacian sailors, past harbors lined with ships, past walls of shining stone — and no one so much as glances at him. At last the girl stops him before the high doors of the palace and tells him plainly: this is the house of Alcinous; go to the queen first, not the king. If Arete looks on him with favor, he will get home. Then, before he can thank her, she is gone.

Scene Two · The Wonder

The most description Homer ever gives a room

Odysseus stops at the threshold and does not move. Even before he steps inside, the palace of Alcinous stuns him into stillness — Homer spends more lines describing this single building than almost any other place in the entire poem.

The walls run left and right of the door, sheathed in bronze from end to end, capped with a cornice of deep-blue enamel. The doors themselves are gold, hung on silver doorposts that rise from a bronze threshold, with a silver lintel above and a golden ring set in the door to pull it shut.

On either side of the entrance stand dogs of gold and silver — not living animals but statues, forged by the smith-god with all his skill, set there to guard the house of Alcinous forever, ageless and undying.

“Gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made with all his cunning craft, to guard the palace of great-hearted Alcinous — immortal things, and ageless, all their days.”

Inside, golden statues of young boys stand along the walls, each holding a blazing torch to light the hall through the long feasts held there night after night. Fifty serving-women work through the house — some grinding golden grain at the mill, others weaving and spinning wool, their hands as quick and constant as the shimmer of poplar leaves.

And beyond the courtyard gate lies an orchard where the fruit never fails and never rots — pears and pomegranates, gleaming apples, sweet figs and heavy-laden olives, ripening in every season, because the breath of the West Wind blows there always, budding one crop while it ripens the next in endless succession. Two springs feed the garden, one channeled through the orchard, the other running beneath the courtyard to the fountain where the townsfolk draw their water.

    Scene Three · The Supplication

    A stranger appears out of nowhere

    Still cloaked in Athena’s mist, Odysseus crosses the courtyard and enters the great hall, where Alcinous and Arete sit at feast with their lords, golden cups in hand, pouring libations before bed. He walks straight past the king — exactly as instructed — toward the queen.

    The moment he reaches Arete, the mist dissolves. And there, without warning, in the middle of a feast, a strange and filthy man is kneeling at the queen’s knees, clasping them as a suppliant, begging her to grant him safe passage home.

    The hall falls utterly silent. No one moves. A stranger has appeared from nowhere inside their own guarded hall — mist, magic, or simple shock, no one can say — but for a long moment nobody speaks a word.

    “Alcinous, it is not fitting, nor does it become you, that a stranger should sit on the ground, in the ashes at the hearth. Come, lift him up, and give him a seat, and tell the heralds to mix more wine, so that we may pour it out to Zeus, who stands beside every honest suppliant.”

    It is Echeneus, the oldest lord in the hall, who breaks the silence. His words shame the court into motion. Alcinous rises at once, takes Odysseus by the hand, lifts him from the floor, and — in a real gesture of honor — seats him in the chair of his own son, who gladly gives it up. Bread, meat, and wine are set in front of the stranger before anyone even asks his name.

    Scene Four · Arete’s Question

    Who gave you those clothes?

    The feast winds down. One by one, the Phaeacian lords finish their wine and go home to bed, until only three people remain by the dying fire: Alcinous, Arete, and the stranger.

    It is Arete who speaks first. She has been watching him carefully all evening, and something bothers her. She recognizes the cloak and tunic he is wearing — she wove them herself, with her own handmaidens. This stranger is dressed in clothes that should not exist outside her own household.

    “Stranger, I will ask you this myself, first: who are you, and where do you come from? And who gave you those clothes? Did you not say you came here wandering over the sea?”

    It is the most dangerous question anyone has asked him since he washed ashore. Odysseus has to answer — and how he answers will decide whether this palace stays a place of safety, or becomes a fresh kind of danger.

    The Queen’s Question

    You are Odysseus
    Arete recognizes the clothes. She asks who you are and where you got them. How do you answer?

    Odysseus answers carefully. He tells her the truth — Calypso’s island, seven years of captivity, the raft, Poseidon’s storm that broke it apart, three days adrift, and Nausicaä’s kindness at the river mouth. All of it verifiable, none of it dangerous. But when he reaches the part about the wagon, he changes one detail: Nausicaä had told him to follow at a distance to protect her own reputation. Odysseus tells Arete that he chose to walk behind the wagon — his decision, not the princess’s — so that no shadow of gossip falls on the girl who saved his life. He does not give his name.

    Alcinous listens, deeply impressed — so impressed that, before Odysseus can stop him, he offers Nausicaä’s hand in marriage on the spot, along with a house and riches, if only the stranger will agree to stay. Odysseus deflects the offer as gently as he can. He still has a wife of his own, waiting twenty years, on an island far across the sea.

    Scene Five · The Reckoning

    What has the queen already guessed?

    Five questions from the mist, the threshold, and the question no one can dodge forever.

    The stranger has been lifted from the ashes, seated in a prince’s chair, and fed. He has told enough truth to earn a bed for the night. But the queen is watching, and the question he has dodged — who are you? — will not wait forever.
